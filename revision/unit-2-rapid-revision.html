Scientific Feeding · Feeding Experiments · Experimental Design

WHY AND HOW WE TEST A FEED

Importance of scientific feeding

Definition: feeding animals on the basis of their measured nutrient requirements and the known nutritive value of available feeds, rather than by custom or convenience.

Write these 8 justifications: ① Feed is 60–70% of the total cost of livestock production — the only input where efficiency directly decides profit. ② Ensures the genetic potential of improved breeds is actually expressed. ③ Prevents both under-nutrition (delayed puberty, anoestrus, low yield, low immunity, high neonatal mortality) and over-nutrition (fatty liver, dystocia, ketosis, acidosis, laminitis). ④ Avoids deficiency and toxicity disorders. ⑤ Permits use of cheap, locally available and unconventional feeds without loss of production. ⑥ Improves feed conversion efficiency and reduces nutrient excretion (N, P, methane) into the environment. ⑦ Guarantees product quality — milk fat, SNF, meat quality, shell strength. ⑧ Makes livestock enterprises predictable and bankable.

Indian context line worth adding: our animals live largely on crop residues (paddy and wheat straw) with a national deficit of ~11% green fodder, ~23% dry fodder and ~29% concentrate. Scientific feeding here means strategic supplementation — urea-treated straw, mineral mixture, bypass nutrients — not imported feeding models.

Feeding experiments — six types, and what each one can prove

Also name: in-vitro (Tilley & Terry two-stage), in-sacco (nylon bag), gas production (Menke) and mobile bag techniques as laboratory substitutes for whole-animal trials — cheap, fast, repeatable, but they cannot measure intake or animal response.

Experimental designs & the rules that make a trial valid

DesignWhen it is used
CRD (Completely Randomised)Uniform animals; simplest; unequal group sizes allowed
RBD (Randomised Block)Animals blocked by weight, age, parity or yield, then randomised within block — the commonest livestock design
Latin SquareEach animal receives every treatment in a different order; controls both animal and period effects; needs treatments = periods = animals
Switch-over / Reversal / Change-overLactating cows — each animal is its own control; excellent when animals are few and costly
Split-plot / FactorialTwo or more factors tested together (e.g. energy level × protein source) with interaction

Non-negotiable rules: ① Replication — never one animal per treatment. ② Randomisation — removes bias. ③ Local control — uniform housing, management and climate. ④ A genuine control group. ⑤ Uniform age, sex, weight, breed and physiological status. ⑥ Adequate pre-experimental (adaptation) period. ⑦ Accurate individual feeding and recording. ⑧ Statistical analysis before any conclusion is claimed.

Latin square caution: because the same animal receives successive treatments, a carry-over (residual) effect can confound results — always insert an adequate wash-out/adaptation between periods.

Voluntary intake — the factor that decides everything

Nutrient supply = digestibility × voluntary intake. A highly digestible feed the animal will not eat is nutritionally useless — say this whenever intake is asked.

Regulation of intake: Physical / distension control dominates on roughage diets — rumen fill and rate of passage; correlates negatively with NDF. Chemo-metabolic control dominates on concentrate diets — VFA (especially propionate) receptors, blood glucose, hepatic oxidation, leptin. Thermostatic control — heat increment reduces intake in hot weather (Indian summer, buffalo).

FactorEffect on intake
NDF / lignification of the roughage↑ NDF ⇒ ↓ intake (rumen fill)
Chaffing, grinding, pelleting↑ intake (faster passage)
Moisture > 70% / poor silage↓ intake
Dietary CP below ~7–8%↓ microbial activity ⇒ ↓ intake sharply

Palatability, frequency of feeding, TMR↑ intake

DM intake thumb rules (% of body weight): adult cattle 2.5–3.0 · buffalo 2.5 · high-yielding crossbred cow 3.0–3.5 · sheep & goat 3–4 · growing calf 2.5–3.0.

THE MOST-ASKED LONG ANSWER IN UNIT 2

Conducting a digestion trial — the protocol, in exam order

SELECT ANIMALSmin 3–5, male, uniform
→
PRELIMINARY PERIOD7–14 days
→
COLLECTION PERIOD5–10 days
→
ANALYSISproximate on feed, residue & faeces

1. Animals: minimum 3–5 healthy adults of the same species, breed, age, sex and body weight, dewormed and free from disease. Males (or castrates) are preferred — in females urine contaminates the faeces and separation is difficult.

2. Housing: individual metabolism stalls / digestion crates with separate arrangements for faeces and urine; or faecal collection harness bags for grazing/large animals.

3. Preliminary (adaptation) period — 7–14 days: purpose is to (i) accustom the animal to the ration, stall and handling, (ii) allow the rumen microbial population to adapt to the new feed, and (iii) clear the digestive tract completely of the previous feed. Longer for roughage diets and for ruminants than for pigs and poultry.

4. Collection period — 5–10 days (usually 7): at a fixed hour daily — weigh the feed offered and the residue left; collect total faeces of 24 h, weigh, mix thoroughly and take a representative aliquot of 1/10th (10%); do the same for urine.

5. Preservation: faeces — add toluene or a few drops of formalin/conc. H₂SO₄ to stop fermentation and N loss; store in a cool place; dry at 60–70 °C (low temperature, to avoid losing volatile N). Urine — collect in a bottle containing 20–25 mL of conc. H₂SO₄ or HCl to hold pH below 3, preventing loss of ammonia and bacterial breakdown of urea.

6. Analysis & calculation: run proximate analysis on the composite feed, residue and faeces samples, then compute the digestibility coefficient of each nutrient, and from these DCP and TDN.

Digestibility coefficient (%) = [(Nutrient in feed − Nutrient in faeces) ÷ Nutrient in feed] × 100
DCP % = CP % × DC of CP ÷ 100 · TDN % = %DCP + %DCF + %DNFE + (%DEE × 2.25)
Why the value is called "apparent" digestibility: faeces contain not only undigested feed but also metabolic faecal matter — shed gut epithelium, digestive enzymes, bile, and dead microbes. Subtracting these gives true digestibility, which is always higher than apparent digestibility. The difference is largest for protein and at low levels of intake.

Digestion trial vs Metabolism (balance) trial

PointDigestion trialMetabolism / balance trial
CollectedFeed + faeces onlyFeed + faeces + urine (± milk, ± gases)
MeasuresDigestibility, DCP, TDN, DERetention of a nutrient — N, Ca, P, energy balance
Tells youHow much was absorbedHow much was utilised & stored
LevelDE levelME and NE level
N balance = N intake − (Faecal N + Urinary N + Milk N)

Positive balance = growth, pregnancy, recovery, wool/milk synthesis. Negative balance = starvation, fever, protein deficiency, early-lactation high yielder. Zero (equilibrium) = maintenance.

Sources of error in any trial: incomplete faecal collection; contamination with urine, hair, bedding or soil; loss of volatile N during drying; unrepresentative sampling and aliquoting; feed residue not accounted; too short a preliminary period; individual animal variation; and a change in intake during the collection period.

Practical tip that scores: always record and analyse the residue (orts) separately — animals selectively refuse the stemmy, high-fibre fraction, so residue composition is not the same as the composition of the offered feed.

Measurement of digestibility — direct, indirect & laboratory methods

MethodPrincipleMeritsDemerits
Direct (total collection)Weigh everything eaten and every gram of faeces voidedMost accurate; reference methodLaborious, needs crates, impossible on pasture or in large herds; animals stressed by confinement
Indirect — indicator / markerAn indigestible marker is used as an internal reference; only grab samples of feed and faeces are needed, not total collectionNo total collection; usable on grazing animals; less labourMarker must be truly indigestible, unabsorbed, uniformly mixed and completely recovered — rarely 100% true
Difference methodDigestibility of a basal ration is measured, then the test ingredient replaces a known part of it; digestibility of the ingredient is obtained by differenceOnly way to value a single ingredient (e.g. an oil cake)Associative effects between ingredients distort the answer
In vitro — Tilley & Terry (1963)Two stages: 48 h with strained rumen liquor + buffer under CO₂, then 48 h with acid pepsinFast, cheap, many samples, good correlation with in-vivoNeeds a fistulated donor animal; no intake data
In sacco — nylon bagWeighed sample in a porous nylon bag suspended in the rumen of a fistulated animal and removed at intervals (0–72 h)Gives the rate and extent of degradation — the basis of RDP/RUP valuesFistulated animals needed; particle loss through pores; no post-ruminal digestion
Gas production (Menke)Volume of fermentation gas produced in vitro over timeKinetics of fermentation; screens for methaneIndirect; gas ≠ nutrient supply
Digestibility % = 100 − 100 × [ (% Indicator in feed ÷ % Indicator in faeces) × (% Nutrient in faeces ÷ % Nutrient in feed) ]

Indicators — learn both lists. Internal (natural to the feed): lignin, acid insoluble ash (AIA / silica), indigestible ADF or NDF, plant chromogen, n-alkanes of plant cuticle wax. External (fed deliberately): chromic oxide (Cr₂O₃) — the classic, given as ~10 g twice daily in gelatin capsules or a paper packet; titanium dioxide (TiO₂); polyethylene glycol (PEG, for liquid phase); rare-earth elements (Yb, Dy); barium sulphate.

Properties of an ideal indicator (5): totally indigestible & unabsorbable · no pharmacological effect on the animal or the microbes · mixes uniformly and passes at the same rate as the digesta · quantitatively recoverable in faeces · easily and accurately estimated in the laboratory.

Factors Affecting Digestibility · Feeding Standards

GUARANTEED 10-MARK QUESTIONS

Factors affecting digestibility of a feed

FactorEffect & the reason
1. Composition of the feed — the single most important factor↑ Crude fibre and especially LIGNIN ⇒ ↓ digestibility. Lignin is indigestible and physically shields cellulose & hemicellulose from microbial enzymes. Every 1% rise in lignin drops DM digestibility appreciably
2. Stage of maturity of the fodderAs the plant matures, cell wall (NDF, ADF, lignin) rises and cell contents fall ⇒ both digestibility and intake fall. Hence "cut at 50% flowering"
3. Species & type of animalRuminants > horse > pig > poultry for fibrous feeds. Ruminants digest crude fibre 2–3× better than pigs; poultry practically not at all
4. Level of feedingFeeding above maintenance speeds the rate of passage ⇒ less time for microbial and enzymatic attack ⇒ digestibility falls by ~2–4% per multiple of maintenance. This is why high yielders on heavy intake digest less efficiently
5. Ratio of nutrients / protein levelDietary CP below 7–8% starves the rumen microbes of N ⇒ fibre digestion collapses. Adding urea or a protein supplement to straw raises the digestibility of the straw itself
6. Associative effect of feedsExcess readily fermentable starch lowers rumen pH and inhibits cellulolytic bacteria ⇒ negative associative effect on roughage digestion
7. Feed preparation & processingChaffing, soaking, alkali/urea treatment, steaming ⇒ ↑ digestibility. Very fine grinding of roughage ↓ digestibility (rapid passage) though it raises intake
8. Fat levelFree oil above 3–4% of DM coats fibre and poisons cellulolytic bacteria ⇒ fibre digestibility falls
9. Anti-nutritional factorsTannins bind protein & enzymes; saponins, oxalates and mycotoxins depress digestion
10. Mineral & vitamin adequacyS, P, Co and trace minerals are needed for microbial growth; deficiency ⇒ ↓ digestion
11. Frequency of feeding & water supplyFrequent small meals stabilise rumen pH ⇒ better fibre digestion; water restriction depresses intake & digestibility
12. Work, age, health & individualityHeavy work and parasitism reduce digestibility; very young and very old animals digest less; individual variation of 2–5% always exists

Feeding standards — history in one table

Definition: a feeding standard is a statement of the quantities of nutrients required daily by an animal of a given species, body weight and level of production, to maintain it in the desired state.

StandardWorker / YearBasis — the identifying phrase

Total nutrientsGrouven, 1859Based on the total (not digestible) nutrients of the proximate analysis — its fatal flaw

Wolff–LehmannLehmann, 1897Wolff's tables revised for level of production (e.g. per litre of milk)

Sequence mnemonic: Thaer → Grouven → Wolff → Lehmann → Kellner → Armsby → Haecker → Morrison → NRC → ARC → Kearl → ICAR. "Total → Digestible → Energy → Factorial → Local."

Comparative evaluation — merits & demerits of the major standards

StandardMeritsDemerits
MorrisonSimple DCP + TDN units familiar to Indian teaching; gives a range (minimum–maximum) with a margin of safety; covers many species and classes in one bookEmpirical, derived from practical American feeding experience rather than from measured requirements; TDN overvalues roughages; not adjusted for tropical breeds or climate
NRC (USA)Based on experimentally determined requirements; frequently revised on new research; expressed in modern units (ME, NEL, MP, individual amino acids and minerals); species-specific committeesGives minimum requirements with no safety margin — must be adjusted for stress; based on temperate breeds, feeds and management; assumes high-quality feeds not available in India
ARC / AFRC (UK)Factorial — separately computes maintenance, growth, pregnancy and lactation and sums them; uses ME and Metabolisable Protein (RDP/RUP), so it is biologically the most sound; accounts for efficiency of utilisation (km, kl, kf)Mathematically complex and not field-friendly; requires feed degradability data most Indian laboratories cannot generate
KearlExplicitly derived for tropical, developing-country ruminants on crop residues; practical unitsData base limited; not breed-specific
ICAR (India)Developed on Indian cattle, buffalo, sheep and goat using Indian feeds; accounts for our roughage-based diets and climate; the standard to quote for Indian conditionsFewer categories than NRC; needs updating for high-producing crossbreds and modern buffalo genetics

The concluding sentence to write: "No single standard is universally applicable — a feeding standard is a guide, not a law. It must always be adjusted for breed, climate, housing, stress, feed quality and individual response, and confirmed by monitoring body condition score, production and health."

Balanced Ration · Characteristics · Rapid-Fire Drill

CLOSING SHEET

Balanced ration — definition & characteristics

Definition (write exactly): A balanced ration is one which supplies all the essential nutrients — energy, protein, minerals, vitamins and water — in the required amount and correct proportion, in a palatable and digestible form, to a given animal for a period of 24 hours, so as to enable it to perform the desired function of maintenance and production efficiently and economically.

Characteristics — the 12-point answer:

① Palatable and acceptable — otherwise intake fails. ② Adequate bulk to satisfy the animal and keep the rumen functioning (never all-concentrate). ③ Slightly laxative, never constipating (include wheat bran, green fodder, molasses). ④ Made of a variety of ingredients — improves amino-acid balance and palatability. ⑤ Economical and built from locally available feeds. ⑥ Free from moulds, mycotoxins, adulterants and anti-nutritional factors. ⑦ Supplies nutrients in the correct proportion — appropriate nutritive ratio for the animal's stage. ⑧ Suited to the species and physiological state (growth, pregnancy, lactation, work). ⑨ Contains adequate green fodder for carotene and B-complex precursors. ⑩ At least one-third of the protein from a leguminous or animal source in young animals. ⑪ Includes mineral mixture ~2% and common salt ~1% of the concentrate. ⑫ Changed gradually — never abruptly — over 7–14 days, to protect the rumen microflora.

Standard Indian concentrate mixture (write this if asked to formulate): Maize/grain 30–35% · Groundnut or mustard cake 25–30% · Wheat bran / rice polish 25–30% · Deoiled cake or pulse chuni 10% · Mineral mixture 2% · Common salt 1%. Target: CP 18–20%, TDN 70% (BIS Type I cattle feed).

Practical Indian thumb rules (state these confidently):

• Maintenance: ~1.5 kg concentrate mixture for an adult cow and ~2 kg for a buffalo, plus roughage ad libitum.
• Milk production: 1 kg concentrate per 2.5 L of cow milk (4% fat) and per 2 L of buffalo milk (7% fat).
• Pregnancy allowance: extra 1–1.75 kg concentrate in the last 2–3 months.
• Green fodder: 20–30 kg/day for an adult cow; dry roughage 4–6 kg.
• Roughage : concentrate ratio — maintenance 80:20, average production 60:40, high yielder 50:50 (never below 40% roughage).

Never write a ration without checking three things: total DM intake is within the animal's capacity (2.5–3% BW), the nutritive ratio suits the physiological stage, and minerals and salt have been added. Examiners deduct marks for a ration that is nutritionally correct but physically impossible to eat.
