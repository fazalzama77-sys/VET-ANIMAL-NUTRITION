THE FRAMEWORK

The ideal protein concept — the modern basis of pig and poultry formulation: express every essential amino acid as a percentage of lysine (lysine = 100), so the diet contains amino acids in exactly the proportion the animal deposits, with no excess to be deaminated. Typical ideal pattern for the pig: Lys 100 : Met+Cys 60 : Thr 65 : Trp 18. Formulate on digestible (ileal) amino acids, not total.

Methods of determining nutrient requirements

MethodPrinciple & comment
FactorialRequirement is built up by adding the separate needs for maintenance, growth, reproduction and production, each divided by its efficiency of utilisation. Logical and flexible; depends on accurate efficiency values
Dose–response / graded levelsGroups are fed graded levels of the nutrient; performance is plotted and the break-point (plateau) read off. The commonest method for amino acids, vitamins and minerals in poultry
Nitrogen (or mineral) balanceThe intake giving zero balance is the maintenance requirement; the level giving maximum retention is the production requirement
Comparative & serial slaughterBody composition of slaughtered groups reveals actual nutrient deposition. Accurate but destructive and costly
Tissue saturation / enzyme & blood assayThe intake at which the tissue level or the dependent enzyme activity plateaus (e.g. plasma vitamin, glutathione peroxidase for Se)
Radioisotope dilutionLabelled nutrient traces turnover, pool size and true absorption
The distinction to state clearly: a requirement is the minimum that prevents deficiency and supports the defined performance; a recommended allowance is the requirement plus a margin of safety for stress, variation in feed composition, bioavailability and processing losses. NRC publishes requirements; BIS and commercial standards publish allowances.

Calorie : protein ratio (kcal ME per kg ÷ % CP) — broiler starter ≈ 130–140, broiler finisher ≈ 150–160, layer ≈ 150–170. Birds eat to satisfy their energy requirement: if the ratio widens (energy too high relative to protein) the bird stops eating before it has taken in enough protein and becomes fat with poor growth or small eggs; if it narrows, expensive protein is burnt as energy. Always change protein and energy together.

Swine Nutrition · Equine Nutrition

TWO CONTRASTING MONOGASTRICS

Swine — the efficient omnivore

Digestive peculiarities: simple stomach, omnivorous, highly efficient converter (FCR 2.5–3.5), but almost no ability to use fibre (keep CF below 6–8% in young pigs). Lysine is the first limiting amino acid in cereal-based diets. Prolific and fast-growing ⇒ nutrition dominates profitability.

Piglet must-knows: ① Iron-dextran 150–200 mg IM on day 3 — sow's milk is iron-poor and confined piglets have no soil contact ⇒ otherwise piglet anaemia. ② Colostrum within 12 h. ③ Creep feed early. ④ Clean water always. Flushing the gilt/sow (extra feed 10–14 days before mating) raises ovulation rate.

Deficiency signs unique to pigs: parakeratosis (Zn, aggravated by high dietary Ca), goose-stepping (pantothenic acid), mulberry heart & hepatosis dietetica (Vitamin E/Se), salt poisoning (water deprivation with normal salt intake), and gossypol toxicity from cottonseed cake.

Equine — the hind-gut fermenter

Digestive peculiarities — the six that explain every feeding rule: ① Small stomach (8–15 L, only ~10% of GIT capacity) ⇒ small frequent meals. ② No gall bladder ⇒ continuous bile flow, poor tolerance of large fat loads. ③ Enormous caecum and colon (~60% of GIT) housing the microbial population ⇒ fibre is fermented after the small intestine, so microbial protein and B vitamins are synthesised too far down to be well used. ④ Therefore dietary protein quality matters (lysine is first limiting) unlike in a ruminant. ⑤ Cannot vomit. ⑥ Grazing animal designed to eat for 16 h a day.

The five golden rules of horse feeding: ① Feed little and often (3–4 meals). ② Roughage first and always — a minimum of 1% of body weight as long-stem forage, ideally 1.5–2%. ③ Make every change gradually over 7–14 days. ④ Water before feeding, and never work a horse immediately after a full feed. ⑤ Feed by weight, not by volume, and keep to a fixed routine. Total DM intake 1.5–3% of body weight.

Nutritional disorders of the horse: Colic (sudden change, mouldy feed, sand, inadequate water) · Laminitis (grain overload, lush pasture — hind-gut acidosis) · Azoturia / "Monday morning disease" / tying-up (full grain ration continued during a rest day, then sudden work) · "Big head" (osteodystrophia fibrosa) from high-oxalate tropical grasses binding calcium · Monensin poisoning — an ionophore intended for cattle is fatal to horses.

Poultry Nutrition — Broilers, Layers, Ducks, Quails & Turkeys

THE LARGEST TOPIC IN UNIT 4

Broiler feeding

Peculiarities of the fowl: no teeth (the gizzard grinds), very rapid passage (~4 h), no urinary separation — urine and faeces void together as uric acid, so a true urinary N balance is impossible; ME is used, not TDN; requires glycine/serine in addition to the ten essentials; needs Vitamin D₃ (D₂ is only ~1/10 as potent); synthesises its own Vitamin C.

PhaseAgeCPME kcal/kg
Pre-starter0–7 d23–24%2900–3000
Starter0–3 wk23%2800–3000
Finisher4–6 wk20%3000–3200

Performance benchmarks: market weight 1.8–2.2 kg at 5–6 weeks; FCR 1.5–1.7; mortality <5%. Feed is offered ad libitum as crumbles then pellets.

Key nutritional points: methionine is the first limiting amino acid in maize–soya diets, lysine second; energy is raised through the phases with added fat/oil; coccidiostat is included and must be withdrawn before slaughter; Ca 1.0% and available P 0.45%; phytase is routinely added to release phytate P.

Ascites (waterbelly) and Sudden Death Syndrome occur because modern broilers grow faster than their cardiopulmonary capacity — controlled by moderating early energy intake and lighting. Skeletal problems (tibial dyschondroplasia, rickets) follow Ca:P imbalance or Vitamin D₃ deficiency.

Intake & output: a layer eats 110–120 g/day and lays an egg whose shell contains about 2 g of calcium — roughly 10% of her entire body calcium every single day. That figure alone explains the whole calcium strategy.

Calcium strategy — the marks-fetching detail: supply part of the calcium as coarse particulate limestone or oyster shell rather than all as fine powder. Coarse particles are retained in the gizzard and dissolve slowly, releasing calcium during the night when shell calcification actually occurs. Feed the calcium-rich portion in the afternoon/evening. Also ensure Vitamin D₃, phosphorus and manganese.

Grower restriction: pullets are restricted in the growing phase to prevent over-fat birds, delay sexual maturity to the target age and achieve uniform body weight at housing — uniformity, not weight, predicts peak production.

Layer disorders to name: Cage layer fatigue (Ca/P/D₃ — osteoporosis and paralysis in caged birds), thin/soft shells (Ca, D₃, heat stress), fatty liver haemorrhagic syndrome (excess energy, choline/methionine deficiency), prolapse (over-fat, early maturity). In heat stress add sodium bicarbonate and raise nutrient density because intake falls.

Unconventional feed ingredients for poultry & swine — name and limit them: de-oiled rice bran, maize gluten, sunflower and safflower cake, sal seed meal (tannins), mahua cake (saponins), salseed / karanj cake (bitter principles), silkworm pupae meal (high protein, rancidity risk), azolla, spirulina, single-cell protein, slaughterhouse and poultry by-product meal, dried bakery waste, molasses, tapioca/cassava (HCN — must be sun-dried), and fruit/vegetable processing waste. The three limits that must always be respected: fibre content, anti-nutritional factors, and consistency of supply.

Rabbit · Laboratory Animals · Dog · Cat · Captive Wild Animals

SPECIES PECULIARITIES

Rabbit & laboratory animals

RABBIT — hind-gut fermenter with a unique trick. Diet: CP 16–18%, crude fibre 12–16% (a minimum of 12% is essential — low fibre causes enteritis and mucoid enteropathy), ME ~2500 kcal/kg, Ca 0.5–1.0%. Fed as pellets plus green fodder.

Caecotrophy (coprophagy) — always asked. The rabbit produces two kinds of faeces: hard, dry pellets voided during the day, and soft, mucus-coated caecotrophs (night faeces) which it takes directly from the anus and swallows whole. These caecotrophs are rich in microbial protein and B vitamins synthesised in the caecum, and re-ingesting them recovers nutrients that would otherwise be lost past the site of absorption. Preventing caecotrophy (e.g. by a collar) causes growth failure — proof that it is nutritionally essential, not a vice.

Rat20–24%Omnivore; practises coprophagy for B vitamins; the classic model for PER and BV assays

General laboratory-animal principles: diets must be chemically defined, batch-consistent, sterilisable (autoclaving destroys vitamins ⇒ over-fortify), and free from contaminants, because the animal's nutrition is an experimental variable. Provide ad libitum clean water and a fixed photoperiod.

Dog & cat

DOG — an omnivorous carnivore. Adult maintenance CP 18–22%, fat 8–15%; puppy/lactating bitch CP 25–30%. Energy: ME ≈ 130 kcal × W0.75 per day for an adult; puppies and lactating bitches need 2–4×. Dogs have no dietary carbohydrate requirement (glucose can be made from protein and glycerol) but tolerate starch well. Requires arginine; some breeds need taurine (deficiency ⇒ dilated cardiomyopathy). Feed adults once or twice daily; large breeds need controlled growth and correct Ca:P — over-supplementing calcium causes developmental orthopaedic disease.

THE CAT — obligate carnivore. The seven peculiarities (the single most-asked question in Unit 4):
① High protein requirement — adult 26–30%+, kitten 30–35%; hepatic transaminase and urea-cycle enzymes are permanently high and cannot be down-regulated, so protein is catabolised for energy even during fasting.
② Taurine is dietary-essential — cats have low cysteine sulphinic acid decarboxylase and conjugate bile acids only with taurine. Deficiency ⇒ feline central retinal degeneration (blindness), dilated cardiomyopathy, and reproductive failure.
③ Arginine is critically essential — a single arginine-free meal can cause hyperammonaemia, tremors and death, because the cat cannot synthesise ornithine.
④ Cannot convert β-carotene to Vitamin A (no intestinal dioxygenase) ⇒ needs preformed retinol from animal tissue.
⑤ Cannot convert tryptophan to niacin ⇒ requires about 4× the dog's dietary niacin.
⑥ Arachidonic acid is essential — the cat lacks Δ6-desaturase and cannot make it from linoleic acid.
⑦ No sweet taste receptor and limited glucokinase ⇒ indifferent to sugars and poorly adapted to high-carbohydrate diets.

Feline clinical links: hepatic lipidosis in an obese cat that stops eating; feline lower urinary tract disease / struvite urolithiasis — controlled by urinary acidification, restricted magnesium and, above all, increased water intake (wet food). Cats are nibblers — offer several small meals.

Feeding wild & captive zoo animals

The four governing problems in captivity: ① the natural diet is unknown or unobtainable, so we feed an approximation; ② activity is greatly reduced ⇒ obesity is the commonest nutritional disorder in a zoo; ③ animals are exposed to unnatural feeds and to visitor feeding; ④ deficiencies appear slowly and are often diagnosed only at post-mortem.

GroupFeeding practice & the classic pitfall
Large carnivores (tiger, lion, leopard)8–10 kg meat/day for a tiger, with bone — muscle meat alone is severely Ca-deficient (Ca:P about 1:20) and causes nutritional secondary hyperparathyroidism / rickets. Give one fasting day per week to mimic natural feeding and prevent obesity and gut problems. Supplement Vitamin A, taurine and calcium
Fish-eating species (otter, penguin, seal, piscivorous birds)Raw fish contains thiaminase ⇒ thiamine deficiency; supplement thiamine and Vitamin E (fish oil oxidation causes steatitis / yellow fat disease)
Herbivores (elephant, deer, antelope, rhino)Elephant: 150–200 kg green fodder + 10–20 kg concentrate daily. Provide browse and long fibre for gut health and to prevent stereotypy; mineral licks
PrimatesRequire dietary Vitamin C like the guinea pig; provide fruit, vegetables and a balanced primate pellet; avoid feeding only fruit (low protein, high sugar ⇒ obesity and diabetes)
ReptilesMetabolic bone disease is the classic captive disorder — from low Ca, inverted Ca:P and, crucially, absent UV-B light which reptiles need to synthesise Vitamin D₃. Provide UV-B, calcium dusting and gut-loaded insects
Psittacine & other birdsAn all-seed diet is deficient in Ca, Vitamin A, lysine and methionine ⇒ feather picking, hypovitaminosis A, poor bone. Feed formulated pellets plus greens

General zoo principles to write: mimic the natural diet, feeding frequency and feeding behaviour; use environmental/feeding enrichment (scatter feeding, whole carcass, puzzle feeders) because feeding behaviour is a welfare need, not just nutrition; maintain strict hygiene and feed quality; monitor body weight and condition routinely; adjust for season, breeding and hand-rearing; and keep species-wise diet charts with periodic veterinary review.
