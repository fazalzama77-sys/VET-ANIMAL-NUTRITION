Nutrient Requirements — Maintenance, Growth, Reproduction, Milk, Wool, Draft

THE FACTORIAL BACKBONE OF PAPER II

Maintenance — the base on which everything is added

Definition: the maintenance requirement is the quantity of nutrients needed by an animal to keep body weight, body composition and nitrogen balance constant, with no production and no work, in a thermoneutral environment.

How it is measured — 3 methods: ① Fasting catabolism / basal metabolism — heat produced by a fasted, resting, thermoneutral animal (fasting heat production is the true zero). ② Feeding trial / regression — feed graded levels and find the intake at which weight change and N balance are zero. ③ Comparative slaughter — carcass energy compared against a baseline group.

Metabolic Body Size = W0.75 (Kleiber's law — energy needs scale with surface area, not body weight)
Fasting Heat Production ≈ 70 kcal × W0.75 per day · ME for maintenance ≈ 125 kcal × W0.75

Why W0.75 and not W: heat is lost through the body surface, and surface area is proportional to W0.67–0.75. A 40 kg calf therefore needs far more energy per kg body weight than a 400 kg cow — the single most useful concept in this unit.

Practical ICAR-type maintenance figures for cattle: approximately 0.65 g DCP and 7 g TDN per kg body weight per day. So a 400 kg cow ≈ 260 g DCP + 2.8 kg TDN; a 500 kg buffalo ≈ 325 g DCP + 3.5 kg TDN. Field thumb rule: ~1.5 kg concentrate for a cow, ~2 kg for a buffalo, plus roughage ad libitum.

Factors that raise the maintenance requirement: cold or heat stress, exercise and grazing distance (grazing adds 25–50% over stall feeding), disease and parasitism, poor housing, high milk yield (larger visceral organs), age and breed (Bos indicus have a lower maintenance requirement than Bos taurus — a real advantage in India).

The factorial principle — write this equation in any "requirement" answer:

Total requirement = Maintenance + Growth + Pregnancy + Lactation + Work

Each component is computed separately from the net requirement of the tissue, then divided by the efficiency of utilisation (km ≈ 0.72 maintenance, kl ≈ 0.62 lactation, kf ≈ 0.45 fattening) to convert net to metabolisable requirement.

Nutrient partitioning & priority — the concept examiners love

When nutrient supply is limited, the body does not share it equally. The homeorhetic priority order is:

1. MAINTENANCEsurvival first
→
2. GROWTHskeletal
→
3. LACTATIONspecies survival
→
4. RESERVESfat
→
5. REPRODUCTIONfirst to be sacrificed

The clinical consequence: reproduction is the last to be served and therefore the first indicator of under-nutrition. This is exactly why an under-fed herd shows anoestrus, repeat breeding and long calving intervals long before milk yield visibly drops. In early lactation the cow deliberately mobilises body reserves for milk even at the cost of fertility — the physiological basis of negative energy balance, ketosis and the post-partum anoestrus of Indian crossbred cows.

Body Condition Score (BCS, 1–5 scale): at calving 3.0–3.5; at peak lactation not below 2.5; at drying-off 3.0–3.5. A loss of more than 1 BCS unit in early lactation predicts ketosis and infertility. BCS is the cheapest field tool for judging whether your feeding standard is actually working.

General principles of ration computation

Steps (write in order): ① Note the animal's species, body weight, age, physiological state and level of production. ② Read the nutrient requirement (DCP, TDN/ME, Ca, P, DM) from ICAR/NRC tables. ③ Estimate DM intake capacity (2.5–3% BW). ④ List locally available feeds with their composition and cost. ⑤ Fix the roughage : concentrate ratio. ⑥ Compute the concentrate mixture to fill the gap. ⑦ Add mineral mixture 2% and salt 1%. ⑧ Check total DM, NR, Ca:P and cost; adjust.

MethodHow it works & when to use
Pearson's SquareBalances one nutrient between two feeds/groups. Desired % in the centre, ingredient values at the corners; subtract diagonally and read parts. Simple, exam-friendly, but only one nutrient at a time
Trial and errorIngredients are put together, totals computed, then adjusted repeatedly. Flexible, handles many nutrients; tedious

Calf feeding & early weaning

Colostrum — the non-negotiable numbers: feed within 30 minutes to 2 hours of birth, at 10% of body weight per day in 2–3 feeds, for the first 3–5 days. Reason: the calf is born agammaglobulinaemic (epitheliochorial placenta blocks antibody transfer), and intestinal absorption of intact IgG closes by 24–36 hours ("gut closure"), with efficiency already halved by 12 h.

Colostrum vs normal milk: higher in immunoglobulins (IgG), total protein, vitamins A, D and E, minerals, and it is laxative (helps expel meconium); lower in lactose. It is roughly 5× the protein and 10× the Vitamin A of normal milk.

Milk feeding: whole milk at 1/10th of body weight daily, gradually reduced. Milk replacer (CP 22–24%, fat 10–15%) can substitute after 3 weeks — must be milk-protein based, since the young calf cannot digest plant starch or non-milk protein.

Calf starter: CP 20–22%, TDN 70–75%, palatable and highly digestible; offered from 2 weeks of age.

Early weaning — the mechanism to write: the rumen develops in response to VFA — especially butyrate and propionate — produced from fermenting the dry starter, not from milk (milk bypasses the rumen through the oesophageal groove). Hence dry feed, not milk, grows the rumen papillae. Wean when the calf eats 1–1.5 kg of starter daily, usually at 6–8 weeks. Advantages: saves saleable milk, cheaper rearing, earlier rumen development, less scours, easier management.

Calf scours prevention: clean utensils, fixed feeding time, milk at body temperature (~38 °C), never over-feed, never sudden change. Provide clean water from week 1 — water is essential for rumen fermentation and cannot be replaced by milk.

Heifer · Pregnant · Lactating · Dry · Bull

Growing heifer: aim for a steady ADG of 400–500 g (crossbred) so she attains 60–65% of adult body weight at breeding and calves at 24–30 months. Do not over-feed energy — excess fat is deposited in the udder as fatty udder / mammary adipose infiltration, permanently reducing lifetime milk yield. Under-feeding delays puberty and produces a small-framed, low-yielding cow.

Pregnant animal & "steaming up": in the last 6–8 weeks give an extra 1–1.75 kg of concentrate to (i) meet rapid foetal growth (60–70% in the last trimester), (ii) build body reserves for the coming lactation, (iii) develop the udder, and (iv) improve calf birth weight and colostrum quality. Avoid over-conditioning — a fat cow at calving means dystocia, fatty liver, ketosis and retained placenta.

Challenge / lead feeding: begin about 2 weeks before calving, increasing concentrate by ~0.5 kg per day up to about 1% of body weight, and continue increasing after calving as long as the cow responds with more milk. Purpose: adapt rumen papillae and microflora to the high-grain lactation diet before the demand arrives, and let the cow express her full peak. Every extra 1 kg at peak yield ≈ 200 kg extra milk over the lactation.

Lactating cow: peak yield at 6–8 weeks but peak DM intake only at 10–12 weeks — this lag creates the inevitable negative energy balance of early lactation. Feed high-energy-density rations, bypass fat, and 4–6 small concentrate meals rather than two large ones.

Dry period — 60 days: allows udder tissue regeneration, replenishment of body reserves and foetal growth. A dry period shorter than 45 days cuts the next lactation by 20–25%. Feed mainly roughage; keep Ca low in the last 2–3 weeks to prime the milk-fever defence.

Breeding bull: maintenance ration plus 1–1.5 kg extra concentrate; keep him in breeding, not fattening, condition. Ensure Vitamin A, Zn, Se and adequate exercise — obesity causes low libido, poor sperm motility and leg problems.

Working bullock: energy is the limiting nutrient. Feed the extra ration after work, not before; give ample water and salt; add 25–100% over maintenance depending on work intensity.

Phase feeding of the ewe/doe — the four terms you must define:

• Flushing — extra 250–500 g of concentrate (or improved grazing) beginning 2–3 weeks before mating and continuing 2–3 weeks into the breeding season. Purpose: raise body condition and ovulation rate ⇒ more twins and a tighter lambing.
• Steaming up — extra concentrate in the last 6 weeks of gestation, when 70% of foetal growth occurs; prevents pregnancy toxaemia and gives heavier, more viable lambs with better colostrum.
• Creep feeding — a palatable 18–20% CP creep ration offered to lambs/kids from 2–3 weeks in an enclosure the dam cannot enter; speeds rumen development and weaning weight.
• Fattening / finishing — high-energy ration (concentrate 60–70%) for the last 60–90 days before slaughter.

Indian field realities: pregnancy toxaemia in twin-bearing Marwari and Muzaffarnagri ewes on poor late-gestation grazing; urolithiasis in male sheep and goats on high-concentrate, high-P, low-water diets — prevent with Ca:P 2:1, 1–2% ammonium chloride, up to 4% salt and abundant water; and copper toxicity when sheep are fed cattle mineral mixture (never interchange them).

Bypass nutrients — the high-yielder technology

The problem: in a high-yielding cow, rumen fermentation destroys exactly what she needs most. Good-quality dietary protein is degraded to ammonia and rebuilt into microbial protein of fixed, average quality, while dietary fat is biohydrogenated and, above 3–4% of DM, poisons the fibre-digesting bacteria. The animal's genetic potential exceeds what rumen fermentation alone can supply.

BYPASS (RUMEN-PROTECTED) PROTEIN — definition: dietary protein that escapes rumen degradation and is digested and absorbed as amino acids in the small intestine (= RUP or UDP).

How it is made: chemical — formaldehyde treatment at ~1 g HCHO per 100 g of crude protein (0.8–1.2%), forming reversible methylene bridges that are stable at rumen pH 6.5 and hydrolysed in the acidic abomasum; also tannic acid. Physical — controlled heating, roasting, expeller/extrusion. Natural — feeds inherently high in RUP: fish meal, maize gluten, distillers' grains.

Typical Indian practice: formaldehyde-treated mustard cake or groundnut cake fed to crossbred cows and Murrah buffaloes. Benefits: milk yield up 10–15%, higher milk protein, improved N efficiency, less urinary N loss, better body condition and earlier resumption of cyclicity.

BYPASS FAT: calcium salts of long-chain fatty acids (Ca-LCFA) or hydrogenated prilled fat — inert at rumen pH, dissociating in the abomasum. Fed at 100–150 g/day. Raises the energy density of the ration without depressing fibre digestion or causing acidosis — the single best tool against early-lactation negative energy balance.

The balance to state: optimum RDP : RUP is about 65 : 35 for high yielders. Excess bypass protein is wasteful and starves the microbes of ammonia; too little leaves the cow short of intestinal amino acids. Bypass protein is useless unless the rumen is first given enough RDP and fermentable energy.

Non-protein nitrogen (NPN) for ruminants — use, limits and toxicity

PointDetail to write
What is NPNNitrogenous compounds that are not true protein: urea, biuret, ammonium salts, amides, amines, nitrates, and the natural NPN of green fodder and silage

UREA (AMMONIA) TOXICITY — the full answer. Cause: overdose, sudden introduction, uneven mixing, or feeding without fermentable energy ⇒ NH₃ released faster than the microbes can use it. Mechanism: rumen pH rises above 7.5–8.0; ammonia now exists in the un-ionised (NH₃) form, which is freely absorbed across the rumen wall; blood ammonia exceeds the liver's urea-cycle capacity (> ~1 mg/dL) and is neurotoxic. Signs (onset 20 min – 4 h): uneasiness, muscle tremors, salivation, bloat, grinding of teeth, incoordination, laboured breathing, violent struggling, tetany, and death. Treatment: drench 4–5 litres of 5% acetic acid (vinegar) to acidify the rumen and trap ammonia as the non-absorbable NH₄⁺ ion, plus large volumes of cold water to dilute and slow fermentation; repeat as needed; trocarisation for bloat. Lethal dose ≈ 0.3–0.5 g urea per kg body weight.

The unifying principle — write this as your conclusion

Every production disease above is the same story: the modern high-yielding animal's output demand at a single moment exceeds the rate at which her homeostatic machinery can supply the nutrient — calcium at calving, glucose at peak lactation, buffering capacity at grain overload, magnesium on lush pasture, phosphorus in the freshly calved buffalo. They are therefore diseases of production, not of infection, they cluster around the transition period (3 weeks before to 3 weeks after calving), and they are all prevented by feeding management rather than cured by drugs. The three interventions that prevent most of them are: correct dry-period feeding (BCS 3.0–3.5, low Ca, negative DCAD), gradual dietary transition with challenge feeding, and adequate effective fibre with buffers.

CLOSING SHEET

Feeding during drought, flood & scarcity

Principle: in scarcity the objective changes from production to survival — keep the breeding nucleus alive and pregnant, accept the loss of milk yield.

Strategies to list: ① Survival ration — as low as 50–60% of maintenance for short periods; feed at least 1% of body weight as roughage to keep the rumen functional. ② Urea-ammoniated straw (4 kg urea + 40 L water per 100 kg straw, 21 days). ③ UMMB licks for grazing animals. ④ Unconventional and agro-industrial by-products — sugarcane bagasse and tops, tree leaves (subabul, neem, babul, pipal), cactus, seaweed, molasses, deoiled cakes, vegetable and fruit waste, poultry droppings (where permitted). ⑤ Complete feed blocks / densified total mixed ration blocks — easy to transport to a scarcity zone. ⑥ Culling unproductive stock to save feed for the productive core. ⑦ Fodder banks, hay and silage reserves made in the flush season. ⑧ Ensure clean water and mineral mixture — deficiency kills faster than hunger.

Flood-specific: feeds get wet and mouldy ⇒ aflatoxin risk; provide dry, elevated storage, toxin binders, and watch for nitrate poisoning from flooded, heavily manured forage.
