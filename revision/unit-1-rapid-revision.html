Write these 4 differences: ① Animal stores energy as fat + glycogen; plant as starch + fructans. ② Plant has a cell wall (cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin); animal has none. ③ Animal sterol = cholesterol; plant = phytosterol. ④ Animal N is almost all true protein; plant carries substantial NPN (nitrate, amides, alkaloids).

Age effect: newborn = water 75–80%, fat ~2%. As age ↑ → water ↓, fat ↑, protein & ash nearly constant. Fat is the most variable body constituent; water the most abundant.

Nutritional terms — 2-mark definitions

Feed/Feedstuff — any edible material furnishing nutrients. Ration — total feed allowed to one animal in 24 h. Diet — feed(s) offered, without a time limit. Balanced ration — a ration supplying all nutrients in required proportion & amount for 24 h to maintain the animal in a specified state of production. Maintenance ration — supports life processes with zero gain, zero loss, zero production. Production ration — over and above maintenance.

Nutrient — a chemical substance that supports life. Dry matter (DM) — feed minus moisture (105 °C). Concentrate — CF < 18% DM, high TDN. Roughage — CF > 18% DM. Supplement — corrects a specific nutrient deficit (protein/mineral). Additive — non-nutritive, added to improve efficiency/quality. Palatability — feed characters that attract the animal & determine voluntary intake. Ad libitum — offered free-choice. TMR — roughage + concentrate mixed so animal cannot select. Provender — ground/crushed dry feed mixture.

NDF ⇒ predicts intake (rumen fill). ADF ⇒ predicts digestibility & energy (lignin). Both ↑ with maturity of the fodder ⇒ both intake and digestibility fall. Cell contents ≈ 98% digestible; cell wall is variably digestible depending on lignification.

NDF = Intake, ADF = Digestibility. "N for Nibble, A for Absorb."

Why nutrients matter — production & health (the opening paragraph for any long answer)

Six nutrient classes: water, carbohydrate, protein, lipid, minerals, vitamins. Functions grouped in 3: ① Energy-yielding (CHO, lipid, protein) ② Body-building / structural (protein, minerals, water, lipid) ③ Regulatory (vitamins, minerals, water, enzymes/hormones). Feed = 60–70% of total cost of livestock production — hence nutrition is the single largest determinant of profitability. Under-nutrition ⇒ delayed puberty, anoestrus, low milk yield, low immunity (T-cell & antibody suppression), high neonatal mortality; over-nutrition ⇒ fatty liver, dystocia, ketosis, acidosis, lameness. Nutrition–immunity–reproduction–production is one continuum — that sentence earns marks in any "importance of nutrients" question.

Carbohydrate · Protein · Lipid · Water — Nutritional Aspects

THE FOUR ORGANIC CHAPTERS

Carbohydrate nutrition

Classification: Monosaccharides (glucose, fructose, galactose, ribose, xylose, arabinose) → Disaccharides (sucrose = glu+fru; lactose = glu+gal, only animal sugar; maltose = glu+glu; cellobiose) → Oligosaccharides (raffinose, stachyose — flatulence factors of soybean) → Polysaccharides: storage = starch (amylose+amylopectin), glycogen, fructans, inulin; structural = cellulose (β-1,4), hemicellulose, pectin, lignin (not a CHO — a phenolic polymer).

Structural (fibrous) vs Non-structural (NSC): the key nutritional divide. Ruminants extract structural CHO via microbial cellulase; monogastrics cannot (no β-1,4 glycosidase) and depend on hind-gut fermentation only marginally (horse/rabbit are the exceptions — large caecum).

Wolin: 57.5 C₆H₁₂O₆ → 65 Acetate + 20 Propionate + 15 Butyrate + 60 CO₂ + 35 CH₄ + 25 H₂O
VFARoughage dietConcentrate dietMetabolic fate

Normal acetate:propionate ≈ 3:1. Feeding ↑ concentrate narrows it → milk fat depression + ↑ body weight gain. VFAs supply 70–80% of the ruminant's energy; methane wastes 6–8% of GE.

Field line: ruminants are functionally glucose-poor — almost no dietary glucose is absorbed; blood glucose 40–60 mg/dL (vs 80–120 in monogastrics) and is made from propionate + glucogenic AA + glycerol.

Protein nutrition

Essential AA (10) — mnemonic:

P V T T I M H A L L — Phenylalanine, Valine, Threonine, Tryptophan, Isoleucine, Methionine, Histidine, Arginine, Leucine, Lysine. Poultry additionally need Glycine/Serine; the cat uniquely requires dietary Taurine and cannot spare Arginine.

Semi-essential: Cysteine spares Methionine; Tyrosine spares Phenylalanine. Limiting AA: Lysine in cereal/maize-based swine diets; Methionine in maize–soya poultry diets; Lysine + Methionine for milk protein in high yielders (hence rumen-protected Met/Lys).

Ruminant protein metabolism — the whole story in one line: dietary CP splits into RDP (degraded to peptides → AA → NH₃) and RUP/bypass (escapes to abomasum). Rumen microbes use NH₃ + carbon skeleton + ATP from fermentable energy to build Microbial Crude Protein (MCP), which alone can meet 50–80% of the animal's protein need and is of high, constant biological value (~66–80). Excess NH₃ → absorbed → liver urea cycle → urine or recycled via saliva & rumen wall (ruminohepatic N recycling — the ruminant's N-conservation mechanism on low-protein straw diets).

Protein equivalent of urea = 45–46.6% N × 6.25 = 281–291% CP

Rumen NH₃-N optimum ≈ 5 mg/100 mL rumen fluid for maximum microbial growth. N : S ratio 10–14 : 1 is mandatory when feeding NPN (S needed for Met, Cys, biotin, thiamine).

Protein quality is a monogastric concept. In ruminants, microbial fermentation levels out AA quality, so BV of the diet matters far less than degradability (RDP:RUP) and total N supply. Write this sentence for "measures of protein quality in ruminants vs non-ruminants".

Lipid nutrition

Classification: Simple (fats/oils = triglycerides; waxes) · Compound (phospho-, glyco-, lipoproteins) · Derived (fatty acids, glycerol, sterols, fat-soluble vitamins).

Functions: most concentrated energy source (2.25× CHO), carrier of vitamins A D E K, source of EFA, structural (cell membrane phospholipids), insulation, precursor of eicosanoids & steroid hormones, improves palatability, reduces dust & ration heat increment.

Essential fatty acids: Linoleic (C18:2, ω-6) — truly essential in all species; α-Linolenic (C18:3, ω-3); Arachidonic (C20:4) — essential only for the cat (no Δ6-desaturase). Deficiency ⇒ scaly dermatitis, poor growth, hair loss, impaired reproduction, ↓ egg size.

Rumen biohydrogenation: dietary unsaturated FA are lipolysed then hydrogenated to stearic acid (C18:0) by rumen bacteria → ruminant depot fat is hard & saturated and dietary PUFA hardly reach tissues. Intermediates include trans-10, cis-12 CLA (the milk-fat-depressing isomer).

Bypass / protected fat: free oil above 3–4% of DM coats fibre and poisons cellulolytic bacteria ⇒ fibre digestion crashes. Solution: calcium salts of long-chain fatty acids (Ca-LCFA) / prilled fat, inert at rumen pH 6.5, dissociating in the acidic abomasum. Fed at 100–150 g/day to high-yielding crossbred cows & Murrah buffaloes to raise energy density without acidosis.

Ketone bodies: acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate, acetone — formed when acetyl-CoA exceeds oxaloacetate supply (negative energy balance) ⇒ bovine ketosis, ovine pregnancy toxaemia, feline hepatic lipidosis.

Water — role, requirement, metabolic water

Functions (write 8): universal solvent & transport medium; medium for all hydrolytic/metabolic reactions; thermoregulation (high specific heat & latent heat of vaporisation); lubricant (synovial, CSF, serous); shock absorber (amniotic, aqueous humour); maintains cell turgor & shape; excretion of urea & toxins; essential for milk (milk is 87% water) and for ruminal fermentation.

Three sources: ① Drinking water ② Feed (preformed) water — green fodder 70–90%, straw 8–10% ③ Metabolic water — from oxidation of nutrients.

Nutrient oxidised (100 g)Metabolic water yielded
Fat107 g
Carbohydrate55–60 g
Protein40–42 g

Fat gives the most metabolic water — the classic MCQ (camel hump, hibernating and desert species; kangaroo rat survives on it entirely).

Requirement (thumb rules): cattle/buffalo 3–5 L per kg DM intake (up to 8 in summer); lactating cow adds ~3 L per L of milk; sheep/goat 2–4 L/kg DM; poultry 2 : 1 water : feed (doubles above 32 °C); pigs 2.5–3 L/kg feed.

Losses: urine, faeces, evaporation (skin + lung), milk, sweat. Water deprivation kills faster than any other nutrient deficit — loss of 10% body water = serious illness, 20% = death.

Quality limits: TDS < 3000 ppm safe for cattle; nitrate-N < 100 ppm; salinity > 7000 ppm ⇒ refusal, scouring, drop in production.

Definitions to write verbatim: GE = heat of complete combustion of a unit feed in a bomb calorimeter in excess O₂. DE = GE − faecal energy (apparent, because faeces contain metabolic faecal matter). ME = DE − (urinary + gaseous/methane energy). NE = ME − heat increment (HI); NE is split into NEm (maintenance) and NEp (production). Heat increment = specific dynamic action: unavoidable heat of digestion, fermentation and nutrient metabolism — a loss in summer but useful for keeping warm in winter (favourite viva twist).

TDN % = %DCP + %DCF + %DNFE + (%DEE × 2.25)
1 kg TDN = 4.409 Mcal DE = 4409 kcal · ME = DE × 0.82 (ruminant)
Digestibility coefficient (%) = [(Nutrient eaten − Nutrient in faeces) ÷ Nutrient eaten] × 100
Nutritive Ratio (NR) = (TDN − DCP) ÷ DCP → express as 1 : x

NR interpretation: narrow (1:3 to 1:5) = protein-rich, for young growing/high-yielding animals; wide (1:8 and above) = energy-rich, for maintenance, fattening & draft. Calorie : protein ratio = kcal ME per kg diet ÷ % CP — a poultry concept; broiler starter ≈ 135–140, layer ≈ 160–175. If it widens, birds eat to satisfy energy and become protein-deficient and fat.

Merit/demerit line: TDN is easy and needs only a digestion trial, but it is a digestible-energy-level value expressed in weight units, so it overestimates roughages by 20–30%. SE and NE account for HI and rank roughages correctly, but need a costly respiration calorimeter.

Calorimetry & C–N balance

DIRECT calorimetry — measures heat produced directly. Types: Atwater–Rosa–Benedict adiabatic respiration calorimeter, gradient-layer calorimeter, and the bomb calorimeter (measures GE of the feed itself). Accurate but expensive, cumbersome, and unsuitable for large animals.

INDIRECT calorimetry — computes heat from respiratory gas exchange (O₂ consumed, CO₂ & CH₄ produced) and urinary N.

RQ = CO₂ produced ÷ O₂ consumed → CHO 1.00 · Protein 0.80 · Fat 0.70

Two circuits: Closed-circuit (Regnault–Reiset) — animal rebreathes; CO₂ absorbed by KOH, O₂ replenished and measured. Open-circuit — outside air drawn through, in-and-out gas concentrations analysed (used for large ruminants; today as head-hoods and SF₆ tracer). Comparative slaughter technique — an indirect method comparing carcass energy of a slaughtered baseline group with the fed group; no calorimeter needed, but destructive.

Carbon–Nitrogen balance — indirect method to know what was retained, not just how much: N retention ⇒ protein gain; the carbon left after accounting for protein ⇒ fat gain.

N retained = N intake − (faecal N + urinary N) · Protein gained = N retained × 6.25
C in protein = 52% · C in fat = 76.5% · N in protein = 16%

Worked logic: retained C − (protein gained × 0.52) = C stored as fat; ÷ 0.765 = kg fat deposited. Positive N balance = growth, pregnancy, lactation recovery; negative N balance = starvation, fever, protein deficiency, and the early lactation high-yielder.

Protein evaluation — every formula, with what each one really measures

Apparent Digestibility % = [(N intake − Faecal N) ÷ N intake] × 100
True Digestibility (TD) % = {[N intake − (Faecal N − Metabolic Faecal N)] ÷ N intake} × 100
Biological Value (BV) = [N retained ÷ N absorbed] × 100 = {I − (F − MFN) − (U − EUN)} ÷ {I − (F − MFN)} × 100
Net Protein Utilisation (NPU) = BV × TD ÷ 100 = N retained ÷ N intake × 100
Protein Efficiency Ratio (PER) = Body weight gain (g) ÷ Protein consumed (g)
Net Protein Ratio (NPR) = (Gain of test group + Loss of protein-free group) ÷ Protein consumed
Chemical Score = (mg limiting AA per g test protein ÷ same in whole egg) × 100
DCP % = CP % × digestibility coefficient of CP ÷ 100

How to distinguish them in one line each: BV = of the protein absorbed, how much is retained — a quality measure that ignores digestibility. NPU = BV corrected for digestibility, so it grades the protein as fed. PER = a growth-based bioassay, cheap but influenced by intake and maintenance. NPR corrects PER's biggest defect by adding back the maintenance loss of a protein-free control. Chemical score = a purely laboratory (in-vitro) index using whole egg (BV 100, the reference protein) as the standard; it ignores digestibility and availability.

Reference values: whole egg 100 · milk 85–90 · fish meal 75–80 · microbial protein 66–80 · groundnut cake ~55 · maize (zein, Lys+Trp deficient) ~50.

Also classify by: origin (plant/animal/mineral/synthetic), CF content (concentrate <18% vs roughage >18%), moisture (dry vs succulent), and nutritive value. Legume vs non-legume fodder: legumes (berseem, lucerne, cowpea) carry higher CP (15–20%), Ca and carotene, fix atmospheric N, but cause bloat; non-legumes (maize, oats, Napier) are higher in soluble CHO and safer for silage.

Feed processing — concentrates & roughages

Concentrates — physical: grinding (↑ surface area; too fine causes ulcers in pigs & parakeratosis of rumen), pelleting (↓ wastage & selection, ↑ density & intake, kills Salmonella), crumbling, extrusion & expansion (gelatinises starch — pet foods, aqua feed), steam flaking (↑ starch digestibility in dairy), roasting/popping/micronising, soaking & germination.

Purpose of processing (write 6): ↑ digestibility & palatability · destroy anti-nutritional factors & pathogens · ↓ selective feeding & wastage · ease storage/transport · alter rumen degradability (bypass) · permit inclusion of low-quality ingredients.

Roughage improvement — the 3-way classification:

MethodTechniqueEffect
PhysicalChaffing (2–3 cm), soaking, grinding, pelleting, steam pressure treatment, irradiation↑ intake & ↓ wastage; little change in digestibility
ChemicalAlkali — Beckmann method (1.5% NaOH soak 24 h, then wash) & dry NaOH; Ammonia/urea treatment; Ca(OH)₂; acid; oxidative (SO₂, H₂O₂)Breaks lignin–hemicellulose ester bonds ⇒ DM digestibility ↑ by 8–12 units
BiologicalWhite-rot fungi (Pleurotus, Coprinus) — ligninolytic; fibrolytic enzymes (cellulase, xylanase); solid-state fermentationSelective lignin removal; slow, but no chemical residue
Urea ammoniation of straw — memorise the numbers: 4 kg urea dissolved in 40 L water sprayed over 100 kg straw, stacked airtight (polythene/pit) and cured 21 days; aerate 24 h before feeding. Result: CP rises from 3–4% to 8–10%, DM digestibility ↑ ~10 units, intake ↑ 25–30%. Cheapest single intervention for the Indian straw-based diet.

Conservation — Silage vs Hay

SILAGE = fodder preserved by controlled anaerobic lactic-acid fermentation at high moisture. Ensiling proceeds in 4 phases: ① Aerobic (plant respiration uses residual O₂, temp ↑ 25–35 °C) ② Fermentation (Lactobacillus, Streptococcus, Pediococcus convert sugars → lactic acid, pH falls) ③ Stable/storage (pH < 4.2, all activity ceases) ④ Feed-out/aerobic spoilage (yeasts & moulds on air exposure).

Quality indexGood silage
Crop DM at ensiling30–35%
pH3.8–4.2
Lactic acid3–13% of DM (dominant)
Butyric acid< 0.2%
NH₃-N (as % of total N)< 10%
Colour / smellGreenish-yellow to olive-brown; pleasant acidic-fruity

Ideal crops: high soluble-CHO, low buffering — maize, sorghum, oats, hybrid Napier, sugarcane tops. Legumes ensile poorly (high protein buffering) — wilt them or add molasses. Additives: molasses 1–3%, urea 0.5–1%, salt, formic acid, Lactobacillus inoculants, cellulase. Silos: trench, pit, bunker, tower, and baled/wrapped silage. Butyric (clostridial) silage = rancid, pH > 5, NH₃-N high — caused by wet, soil-contaminated, poorly compacted material. Flieg's score grades silage from the relative proportions of lactic : acetic : butyric acid.

HAY = fodder preserved by drying to <15–20% moisture so enzymes and microbes cannot act. Cut at 50% flowering — the compromise point between yield and quality. Methods: field/swath curing, tripod & barn drying, artificial dehydration. Best hay crops: lucerne, cowpea, oats, Cenchrus, Stylosanthes. Losses: shattering of leaves (the leaf holds most protein & carotene), bleaching (carotene destroyed by sunlight), leaching by rain, and spontaneous heating → fire risk if baled too wet.

One-line comparison: Hay = dry preservation, weather-dependent, carotene lost, cheap storage. Silage = wet preservation, weather-independent, carotene & nutrients retained, needs airtight structure and heavy initial cost.

Additive vs Supplement — the definition question: a supplement supplies a nutrient that the basal ration lacks (protein, mineral, vitamin) and is fed in appreciable quantity; an additive is a non-nutritive substance included in very small amounts to improve feed quality, efficiency of utilisation, animal health or product quality.
